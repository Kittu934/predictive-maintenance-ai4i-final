# Chapter 4 Evaluation and Results

This chapter presents the predictive-performance, computational-efficiency and explainability results generated by the completed implementation. It also evaluates the Streamlit artefact against the required functions and compares the findings with the research reviewed in Chapter 2.

## 4.1 Predictive Performance

Table 6 presents the results obtained from the untouched test set of 2,000 observations, including 68 machine failures.

**Table 6. Test-set predictive performance**

| Model | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1-score | AUC-ROC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Random Forest | 0.980 | 0.719 | 0.676 | 0.697 | 0.961 |
| SVM | 0.971 | 0.586 | 0.500 | 0.540 | 0.955 |
| LSTM | 0.971 | 0.586 | 0.500 | 0.540 | 0.958 |

Random Forest produced the strongest overall result. It achieved the highest accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and AUC-ROC. Its confusion matrix contained 1,914 true negatives, 18 false positives, 22 false negatives and 46 true positives. The model therefore detected approximately 67.6% of the failures while maintaining a comparatively high precision of 71.9%.

SVM produced 1,908 true negatives, 24 false positives, 34 false negatives and 34 true positives. Its AUC-ROC of 0.955 showed that its continuous score ranked failure risk effectively, but its selected operating threshold produced lower recall and F1-score than Random Forest. This indicates that the model separated the classes reasonably well but provided a weaker balance between missed failures and false alarms at the selected threshold.

The LSTM produced the same thresholded confusion matrix as SVM, although its AUC-ROC was slightly higher at 0.958. The difference between AUC-ROC and thresholded F1-score demonstrates why performance should not be judged using one measure alone [12]. The LSTM ranked risk effectively across possible thresholds, but it did not improve the final classification trade-off over SVM.

The findings do not support the assumption that greater architectural complexity necessarily produces superior predictive-maintenance performance. Random Forest was best suited to the structured AI4I benchmark, while the LSTM could not exploit genuine temporal dependencies because the dataset contains independent synthetic observations rather than machine-specific histories [17], [18]. This result is consistent with the critical conclusion in Chapter 2 that deep learning is most advantageous when dataset size and temporal structure match its architectural strengths [3], [6].

## 4.2 Confusion-Matrix Analysis

The false-negative count is particularly important because a missed failure could permit continued operation of deteriorating equipment. Random Forest reduced false negatives from 34 for SVM and LSTM to 22. However, it generated 18 false positives, meaning that some normal observations would be flagged for maintenance.

This trade-off has practical consequences. False negatives can contribute to unexpected breakdown and safety or financial loss, whereas false positives can cause unnecessary inspection and maintenance expenditure [4], [5]. The preferable operating point therefore depends on the relative cost of the two error types. The current threshold was chosen to maximise validation F1-score, but an industrial deployment would require cost-sensitive threshold selection based on equipment criticality and maintenance policy.

**[Insert Figure 6: `outputs/figures/confusion_matrix_random_forest.png`]**

The high overall accuracies must also be interpreted cautiously because 96.61% of the observations represent normal operation. A trivial majority prediction would achieve high accuracy while detecting no failures. Recall, F1-score and the confusion matrices therefore provide more informative evidence than accuracy alone [12].

## 4.3 Computational Efficiency

Table 7 presents median final-model training and inference time together with peak process-memory increase in the experimental environment.

**Table 7. Computational-efficiency results**

| Model | Training time (s) | Inference time for 2,000 cases (s) | Peak memory increase (MB) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Random Forest | 0.402 | 0.046 | 9.105 |
| SVM | 1.376 | 0.039 | 34.398 |
| LSTM | 7.674 | 0.001 | 261.961 |

Random Forest required the least training time and the lowest memory increase. SVM required approximately three times more training time and greater memory, although its test-set inference remained fast. LSTM required the longest training time and substantially more memory because the recurrent model and PyTorch execution stack introduced additional computational overhead [23].

The LSTM recorded the shortest batched inference time. This should not be interpreted as universal evidence that LSTM is faster than the conventional models. The network was small, operated on a single time step and processed the complete test tensor in one batch. In a genuine sequential application with longer windows, larger hidden layers or streaming requirements, its computational behaviour would differ.

## 4.4 Explainability Results

Random Forest impurity-based importance ranked rotational speed, torque and tool wear as the three most influential predictors, followed by air temperature and process temperature. Product Type variables received lower importance values.

**[Insert Figure 9: `outputs/figures/random_forest_feature_importance.png`]**

The Random Forest SHAP summary similarly showed that operational load and condition variables strongly influenced predictions. The agreement between built-in importance and SHAP increased confidence that the model relied principally on meaningful operational variables rather than encoded product identifiers. However, the two methods answer different questions: impurity importance describes aggregate split usage, whereas SHAP estimates the direction and magnitude of feature contributions to individual model outputs [7], [10].

**[Insert Figure 10: `outputs/figures/shap_summary_random_forest.png`]**

The local waterfall explanation demonstrated how feature contributions combined for one actual failure observation. This type of explanation can help a maintenance decision-maker understand why the model produced a high-risk prediction. Nevertheless, SHAP describes the fitted model’s associations and does not prove that changing one variable would causally prevent a failure [10].

SVM and LSTM SHAP summaries were also generated using controlled samples. These explanations improved transparency but required more computation than TreeExplainer. The difference supports the literature’s argument that explainability itself has a practical cost and should be considered alongside accuracy and inference speed [10], [11].

## 4.5 Artefact Evaluation

The dashboard met the principal functional requirements stated by the supervisor. Users can load the default AI4I dataset or upload a compatible CSV file, preview the data, generate predictions, compare the three models, inspect predictive metrics and confusion matrices, view feature importance and SHAP explanations, and examine computational-efficiency results.

Input validation checks required columns, Product Type values and numerical compatibility. The dashboard also provides downloadable predictions and clear warnings about synthetic data, non-causal explanations and the exploratory nature of the LSTM. These features make the artefact more substantial and accessible than a notebook that only trains models and prints scores.

Functional acceptance testing confirmed that the required pages and outputs loaded correctly from the saved project files. However, the absence of a formal representative-user study means that the evaluation cannot establish whether industrial practitioners find the interface intuitive or useful in real maintenance work. The dashboard should therefore be described as a functional research prototype rather than a validated industrial decision-support system.

## 4.6 Comparison with Related Work

The results support Carvalho et al.’s conclusion that predictive-maintenance outcomes depend on dataset, preprocessing and evaluation design rather than one universally superior algorithm [3]. They also align with Zhao et al.’s observation that deep-learning benefits depend on appropriate data volume and structure [6]. Within AI4I, the structured and limited benchmark favoured Random Forest rather than the exploratory LSTM.

Matzka used AI4I to demonstrate the importance of explainability but did not compare Random Forest, SVM and LSTM across predictive performance, computation and SHAP within one artefact [18]. The current project extends that direction by integrating these dimensions into a reproducible dashboard. However, it does not overcome the underlying limitation that AI4I is synthetic. The results add benchmark evidence rather than proof of performance in live manufacturing.

## 4.7 Limitations

Four principal limitations affect the interpretation of the results. First, AI4I is synthetic and does not reproduce all missingness, drift, noise and organisational constraints of real factories [17], [18]. Second, its observations are not genuine machine-specific sequences, restricting the validity of the LSTM comparison. Third, the failure class is small, so performance estimates may change with a different split or external dataset. Fourth, functional dashboard testing does not replace evaluation with representative intended users.

The model comparison also covered only one Random Forest, one SVM and one compact LSTM search design. Broader optimisation could alter results, but an unlimited search would conflict with the project’s achievable MSc scope. The conclusions are therefore limited to the stated dataset, features, search spaces, thresholds and environment.

## 4.8 Chapter Summary

Random Forest provided the strongest balance of predictive performance, computational efficiency and explainability. It achieved an F1-score of 0.697 and AUC-ROC of 0.961 while requiring the least training time and memory. SVM and LSTM achieved similar thresholded performance, although LSTM had a slightly higher AUC-ROC and substantially greater training-memory cost. The Streamlit artefact successfully integrated data selection, model comparison, evaluation, SHAP and efficiency outputs. These findings answer the research questions within the limits of the AI4I benchmark and provide the basis for the final conclusions in Chapter 5.
